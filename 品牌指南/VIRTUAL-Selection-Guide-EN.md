## Selection Guide

1. **Cup size is the first principle**: the cup/probe diameter should be slightly smaller than the outline of the part to be picked — too large leaks air and will not hold, too small gives too little contact area and drops parts. Fix the cup size from the main part sizes first, then the tool body; for stations handling a wide spectrum of parts, stock probe sets across the sizes rather than expecting one cup to do everything.
2. **Choose the power form by workload**: for occasional pick-and-place, inspection and repair, choose a self-contained PEN-VAC or a manual HANDI-VAC (zero maintenance, no hose constraints); for continuous work such as manual SMT placement and rework replenishment, choose the TV-1000 electric system or a benchtop system (constant, adjustable suction, no fatigue over long periods); for mobile stations, choose the battery-powered PORTA series.
3. **On ESD stations, choose ESD versions across the whole set-up**: when handling bare dies, wafer-level packages and precision CMOS devices, both the pen and the cups must be made of static-dissipative materials — human-body static can reach thousands of volts, and the instant a metal tweezer grips a part it can cause gate-oxide breakdown with no visible sign. The TV-1000 controls whole-unit discharge below 50V and meets S20.20, making it the correct configuration for the handling link of the protection chain.
4. **For surface-sensitive parts, choose mark-free / dedicated nozzles**: for optical lenses, bare dies and coated parts, choose mark-free Delrin, PEEK or dedicated contamination-free optical cups; for high-temperature handling, choose the red silicone cup version (resistant to 250°C) or Teflon hard nozzles; ordinary chip parts are fine with Buna-N cups.
5. **Adjustable suction matters more than maximum suction**: 0201-class small parts and 0.5mm-pitch QFPs need stable, fine suction — too much blows small parts away, too little drops them midway. Choose a system with stepless adjustment and set the suction level starting from the smallest part; the TV-1000 with a 0.5mm nozzle achieves a pick-up success rate up to 99% on 0201 parts.

## FAQ

**Q: What is the actual advantage of a vacuum pen over ordinary tweezers?**
A: Three points. First, no gripping — the cup lifts by seating on the surface, so parts are not pinched, scratched or deformed, and bare dies and wafer-level packages can be handled safely. Second, consistency — suction is adjustable and the force constant, so newcomers get up to speed quickly, unlike tweezers, which rely entirely on feel. Third, efficiency — compared with tweezers repeatedly dropping and losing 0.5mm-class small parts, vacuum pickup is about 3 times faster, and the whole process can be brought inside ESD protection.

**Q: The PEN-VAC self-contained pen uses no electricity — where does the vacuum come from, and how much can it lift?**
A: The vacuum is generated manually by the pen's mechanical structure: press the side button to seat on a part, release the button and suction forms, carry the part into place, then press the button again to release — no pump, battery or hose is needed at any point. Its role is flexible pick-and-place of small parts; for continuous high-volume work or heavier parts, step up to the TV-1000 electric system or a benchtop system, where suction is constant and adjustable.

**Q: The cup will not hold and parts keep dropping — what causes it?**
A: Check three things first: whether the cup diameter matches the part outline (it should be slightly smaller than the part); whether the cup has aged, deformed or become contaminated (cups are consumables — once the sealing surface wears, suction drops noticeably and they should be replaced regularly); and whether the part surface is porous, rough or curved (such surfaces seal poorly under vacuum and need a dedicated nozzle or a different handling method). If parts still drop after those three are ruled out, check the system's vacuum level and suction setting.
